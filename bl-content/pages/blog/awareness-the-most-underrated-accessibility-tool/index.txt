<p>This is a true story. The events took place in Matadepera (Barcelona) in 1999. Out of respect for those involved, their names, as well as the nature of my relationship with them, have been changed.</p>
<p>Fred, one of my best childhood friends, who has had severe myopia since he was little, goes to his sports club, as he does every week, to do some swimming. He puts on his swimsuit, takes his glasses off, enters the pool area, and heads toward one end of the pool.</p>
<p>At the other end there’s Tina, a very close friend of mine who works there as a lifeguard. She doesn’t know Fred at all. The pool is undergoing repairs and is therefore only half-full, with at most a metre of water. The pool area is still open, though, because people can use the jacuzzi. Assuming no one would come to swim, Tina takes the chance to read a novel, Siddhartha.</p>
<p>Out of the corner of her eye, she sees Fred entering and heading toward the far end. "He must be going to the jacuzzi over on that side," she thinks, and goes back to her book. Suddenly, she hears a splash, someone has dived into the water. She stands up to see what has happened. Fred is standing in the middle of the pool, the water reaching just below his waist. "What’s going on? Why is there no water?" he asks. "They’re doing repairs, you can’t swim today," Tina replies. Fortunately, Fred has only suffered a minor bump to his head, he’s fine.</p>
<p>Tina told me this story, feeling quite embarrassed. I haven’t seen Fred since we were children. I don’t know his version of events, I don’t know how he experienced it, but I can imagine.</p>
<p>And that, in essence, is what accessibility is also about. No one at that swimming pool thought about Fred and his vision impairment. Yet, making things accessible often doesn’t require major changes or large investments. It’s simply a matter of taking other people’s circumstances into account.</p>
